## Introduction

Meeting a Speech-Language Pathologist (SLP) for the first time can feel both hopeful and uncertain. You may have a clear idea of what you want to change, or you might be unsure where to begin. This article is designed to help you prepare for that first conversation by focusing on one essential skill: describing your goals.

A productive first meeting is not about having all the answers. It is about sharing what matters to you, asking questions, and beginning a collaborative process. The SLP will bring clinical expertise; you bring expertise about your own life, priorities, and daily challenges. When these two perspectives meet, the groundwork for effective services is laid.

This guide offers a practical framework, a checklist, and hypothetical examples to help you communicate your goals clearly. It is not a substitute for individualized advice from a qualified professional. Always consult with a licensed SLP or other appropriate professional for guidance specific to your situation.

## Why Describing Goals Matters

Goals are the compass for any therapy or support plan. Without clear goals, sessions can drift, and progress becomes hard to recognize. When you describe your goals at the first meeting, you help the SLP understand:

- What you or your loved one wants to achieve
- Which daily activities are most affected
- What success would look like in practical terms
- What your priorities and preferences are

This information guides the SLP in asking follow-up questions, suggesting possible next steps, and determining whether their services are a good fit. It also sets the stage for shared decision-making, where you and the SLP work together to refine goals over time.

## Preparing to Describe Your Goals

Before the meeting, spend some time reflecting on what you hope to gain. You do not need to use clinical terminology. Everyday language is best. Consider these areas:

### 1. Identify the Main Concern

What prompted you to seek an SLP? It could be difficulty speaking clearly, understanding others, reading, writing, swallowing, or using language socially. Write down a few words or a sentence that captures the core issue.

### 2. Think About Daily Impact

How does this concern affect daily life? For example:
- At home: difficulty being understood by family members
- At work or school: trouble participating in meetings or classroom discussions
- Socially: avoiding conversations or feeling left out
- Emotionally: frustration, embarrassment, or anxiety

### 3. Envision Your Preferred Outcome

What would you like to be different? Try to describe it in concrete, observable terms. Instead of "I want to speak better," you might say "I want to order food at a restaurant without repeating myself." Instead of "I want my child to talk more," you might say "I want my child to ask for a toy using words instead of pointing."

### 4. Consider Your Priorities

If multiple goals are possible, which matters most right now? For example, you might prioritize being understood on the phone over improving reading skills. There is no right or wrong priority; it is about what fits your life.

### 5. Note Your Questions and Concerns

What do you want to ask the SLP? Examples:
- What does a typical session look like?
- How will we measure progress?
- What can I do at home to support goals?
- How often will we meet, and for how long?

## A Checklist for the First Meeting

Use this checklist to organize your thoughts and ensure you cover key points. You can bring it with you or review it beforehand.

- [ ] Write down the main concern in one or two sentences.
- [ ] List two or three daily situations affected by the concern.
- [ ] Describe what you would like to be able to do instead (your preferred outcome).
- [ ] Rank your top two priorities if there are multiple goals.
- [ ] Note any questions about the SLP's approach, experience, or logistics.
- [ ] Consider bringing a supportive person if that helps you feel comfortable.
- [ ] Be ready to share relevant background (e.g., when the concern started, previous evaluations or services).
- [ ] Ask the SLP how they typically set goals and track progress.
- [ ] Clarify how you prefer to receive information (verbal, written, demonstrations).
- [ ] Discuss any cultural, linguistic, or personal factors that matter to you.

## Hypothetical Examples

These examples are illustrative and not based on real individuals. They show how you might describe goals in everyday language.

**Example 1: An adult who stutters**

"I stutter when I'm nervous, especially at work. I avoid speaking up in meetings because I'm afraid I'll get stuck. My goal is to feel more confident participating in team discussions. I'd like to learn strategies to manage my stuttering so I can share my ideas without holding back."

**Example 2: A parent of a young child**

"My three-year-old uses about 20 words, but mostly points and cries to get what she wants. I want her to be able to tell me what she needs using words. My main goal is for her to ask for things like 'milk' or 'help' instead of getting frustrated. I also want to understand how I can encourage her at home."

**Example 3: An older adult after a stroke**

"Since my stroke, I have trouble finding the right words. I can see the object in my mind but can't say its name. This makes me feel isolated. I want to improve my ability to name everyday items so I can talk with my grandchildren on the phone. I'd also like my wife to learn ways to help me when I get stuck."

**Example 4: A teenager with social communication challenges**

"I have a hard time reading social cues and making friends. I want to learn how to start conversations and keep them going. My goal is to feel less awkward in group settings and maybe join a club at school. I'd like to practice these skills in a safe environment."

## What to Expect from the SLP

The SLP will likely ask questions to understand your goals more deeply. They may ask for examples, clarify what you mean by certain terms, and explore how your goals relate to their scope of practice. They might also explain their approach, suggest possible assessment steps, and discuss how goals could be structured.

Remember that goal setting is a two-way conversation. You are not expected to have a perfect plan. The SLP is there to help you refine your goals into measurable, achievable targets. If something is unclear, ask for an explanation. If you feel rushed, say so. A good working relationship starts with open communication.

## Questions to Ask About Goals

Here are some questions you might ask the SLP to ensure your goals are well understood and addressed:

- How will we decide which goals to focus on first?
- What does progress look like for this type of goal?
- How often will we review and adjust goals?
- What can I do between sessions to support these goals?
- Are there any goals you think I should consider that I haven't mentioned?
- How will you measure whether we're making progress?

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Being too vague:** "I want to talk better" is hard to measure. Try to be specific.
- **Focusing only on the disorder:** You are more than a diagnosis. Share how the concern affects your life.
- **Ignoring your own priorities:** The SLP may suggest goals, but your input is essential.
- **Not asking questions:** If you don't understand something, ask. It's your right.
- **Forgetting to mention practical constraints:** Time, transportation, cost, and availability can affect goal planning. Be honest about these.

## Conclusion

Describing your goals at the first meeting with a Speech-Language Pathologist is a skill you can practice. By reflecting beforehand, using a checklist, and communicating in plain language, you set the stage for a productive partnership. Remember that goals can evolve as you learn more and as your needs change. The first meeting is just the beginning.
