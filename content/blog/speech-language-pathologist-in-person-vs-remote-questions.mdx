# Speech-Language Pathologist: In-Person vs. Remote Appointments — Questions to Ask

Choosing between in-person and remote appointments with a speech-language pathologist (SLP) is a practical decision, not a one-size-fits-all answer. The right format depends on the person receiving services, the goals being worked on, the technology available, and the preferences of everyone involved. This guide is designed to help you ask better questions — not to tell you which option to pick.

This article is informational only. It is not medical, clinical, or legal advice, and it does not diagnose any condition or promise any outcome. If you have concerns about communication, speech, language, feeding, or swallowing, please consult a qualified professional who can evaluate your specific situation.

## Why the Format Question Matters

In-person and remote appointments are different service formats, not simply two ways of doing the same thing. Each has practical trade-offs that are worth discussing openly before you commit to a schedule.

In-person appointments typically involve traveling to a clinic, office, school, or other setting. Remote appointments — often called telehealth or teletherapy — typically happen over a video platform from a location you choose, such as your home.

Neither format is automatically better. What matters is whether the format fits the person, the goals, and the day-to-day realities of your household. The questions below are organized to help you think through those fits.

## A Checklist of Questions to Ask

You can bring this checklist to a first call, a consultation, or an email exchange with a provider. You do not need to ask every question at once, and you do not need to have answers before you start.

### About the Provider and the Format

- Does this provider offer both in-person and remote appointments, or only one format?
- If both are offered, how do they decide which format is recommended for a given situation?
- Are there situations where the provider would recommend switching from one format to the other?
- Who would be the point of contact if questions come up between appointments?

### About the Setting and Logistics

- Where would in-person appointments take place, and what does getting there involve?
- For remote appointments, what platform or equipment is used, and what internet speed or device is needed?
- What happens if a remote session has technical problems partway through?
- Are there quiet-space or privacy considerations for remote sessions at home?

### About Participation and Support

- For remote appointments, is a caregiver, family member, or support person expected to be present?
- For in-person appointments, are caregivers welcome to observe or join part of the session?
- How are home practice suggestions shared, and in what format?
- How does the provider prefer to receive questions or updates between sessions?

### About Scheduling and Continuity

- What days and times are typically available for each format?
- If a session needs to be rescheduled, how is that handled?
- How far in advance are appointments usually booked?
- If the provider is unavailable for a period, what options exist for continuity?

### About Your Own Preferences

- Does the person receiving services seem more comfortable in one setting than another?
- Does your household schedule make one format easier to sustain week to week?
- Are there travel, mobility, or accessibility considerations that affect either option?
- What would make you feel confident that a format is working well — or not working well?

## Clearly Labeled Hypothetical Examples

The following examples are hypothetical illustrations only. They are not real cases, not research findings, and not recommendations. They are included to show how the questions above might apply in different situations.

**Hypothetical example 1: A busy household considering remote appointments.**

Imagine a family with two working caregivers and a school-age child. They are considering remote appointments because driving to a clinic would mean missing part of the workday. In this hypothetical, useful questions might include: What device and internet setup is needed? Is a caregiver expected to sit in for part of the session? What happens if the connection drops? How are home practice ideas shared? These questions help the family picture what a typical week would actually look like.

**Hypothetical example 2: A person who prefers a familiar clinical setting.**

Imagine an adult who feels more focused when they leave the house for appointments. In this hypothetical, the person might ask whether in-person sessions are available, how far the location is, and whether the same provider would be seen consistently. They might also ask what happens if they want to try remote sessions later. These questions help clarify whether the in-person format is realistic to maintain.

**Hypothetical example 3: A caregiver weighing both formats.**

Imagine a caregiver who is unsure which format would work better and wants to compare them directly. In this hypothetical, the caregiver might ask the provider how they typically decide between formats, what signs suggest a switch, and how progress is discussed. The caregiver might also ask whether a short trial of one format is possible before committing.

**Hypothetical example 4: A person with limited internet access.**

Imagine someone whose home internet is unreliable. In this hypothetical, useful questions might include whether a phone-based option exists, whether sessions can be shortened, and whether in-person appointments are available nearby. These questions help surface practical constraints before scheduling begins.

## How to Use What You Learn

After you ask these questions, you will likely have a clearer picture of which format fits your situation — or whether the choice is still close. A few practical steps can help you move forward:

1. **Write down the answers.** Notes make it easier to compare providers or revisit a decision later.
2. **Ask about a trial period.** Some providers may be open to trying one format for a set number of sessions before deciding.
3. **Revisit the decision.** Format needs can change over time. What works in one season may not work in another.
4. **Keep the conversation open.** If something is not working, say so. Providers can often adjust scheduling, setup, or approach.

## A Short Recap

- In-person and remote appointments are different formats with different practical trade-offs.
- The best format depends on the person, the goals, and the household.
- A checklist of questions can help you compare options without pressure.
- Hypothetical examples can help you picture what each format might look like.
- Format decisions can be revisited as needs change.

If you are unsure where to start, begin with a single question: what would make appointments feel manageable and sustainable for you or the person you are supporting? That answer often points toward the format worth exploring first.
